## Campbell's (CPB)

**Initiated 2026-08-18.** Screen data (Morningstar, Aug 14): 5 stars, Medium uncertainty, Wide
moat, FV $56 vs. price $23.17, $6.9B market cap.

**Pillar 1 (margin of safety / moat) — mixed.** Legacy soup: commoditized, high buyer power
from retailer/private-label competition, thin differentiation — a hard position under Five
Forces, not one the business is investing to defend. Rao's: a plausible differentiation-based
moat (brand authenticity, less private-label substitution risk), but unproven at the scale and
price paid ($2.7B, 14.6x/19.8x EBITDA). Net: whatever margin of safety exists rests almost
entirely on Rao's, not the legacy business, and Rao's moat is a thesis, not yet a fact.

**Pillar 2 (transparency) — fails on the specific thing that matters most.** Campbell's folded
Sovos/Rao's into an undisclosed "Distinctive Brands" unit inside Meals & Beverages after close
(March 2024). That specifically prevents setting a clean, checkable Rao's-standalone checkpoint.

**Track record — the actual weak point.** The closest precedent, the older snacks portfolio
(Pepperidge Farm, Goldfish, Kettle, from the 2018 Snyder's-Lance deal), was struggling as of
the initiation date: margin fell 390bp to 7% that quarter, management said recovery was
"taking longer than anticipated." Current, negative evidence on the exact capability the Rao's
bet depends on.

**Forward checkpoints (set 2026-08-18, grade at FY26 Q4, expected ~October 2026):**
1. *Rao's growth rate* — does consumption growth hold near low-double-digits or decelerate
   toward the deal's implied requirement being missed?
2. *Snacks margin trajectory* — sequential recovery toward management's target, or continued
   deterioration? Tests whether this management can fix an underperforming acquired
   branded-food business — directly informs confidence in the Rao's bet by extension.
3. *Disclosure* — does any standalone Sovos/Rao's figure reappear (10-K footnote, investor-day
   breakout)? Reappearance would be a genuine positive signal; continued opacity keeps pillar 2
   failing.
4. *Soup category volume* — does the -8% trend stabilize, worsen, or just track a broader
   category decline (check against IRI/Nielsen-style data, not just Campbell's own number)?
5. *Insider activity / buybacks* — any change in pace at current levels, independent of public
   guidance.

**Initiation verdict (2026-08-18): lean negative.** Pillar 1's margin of safety depends on an
unproven Rao's moat, pillar 2 fails on the one disclosure that matters most, and the closest
read on management's track record for this specific capability is currently negative. Not a
rejection — a "wait for checkpoints 1–2 to resolve before increasing conviction either way."

**Status as of 2026-08-21: unchanged, checkpoints not yet due.** FY26 Q4 hasn't reported;
no new information to grade against. Re-check when Campbell's reports (~October 2026).

## Hershey (HSY)

**Initiated 2026-08-21.** Screen data (Morningstar, Aug 14): 5 stars, Low uncertainty, Wide
moat, FV $230 vs. price $184.22, $37B market cap. Current price as of 2026-08-21: $186.46.
Street consensus is notably more cautious than Morningstar's screen: 24-analyst S&P Global
average target $217.14 (Hold consensus); TipRanks 5 buy/12 hold/0 sell, average $203.13; a
separate aggregator puts the consensus at $204.78 (range $166–$255). That's roughly 9–16%
upside off live consensus, materially less than the ~24% implied by Morningstar's $230 fair
value — a real source-disagreement worth flagging per the framework's source-agreement check,
not a reason to discard the thesis outright.

**Pillar 1 (margin of safety / moat) — real and currently demonstrated, but with a live
crack to watch.** Five Forces: supplier power is high and volatile (concentrated cocoa
origination, an active multi-year cost shock), buyer power is high (Walmart/Kroger-level
retail concentration), barriers to entry are high (brand equity, retail distribution scale,
occasion ownership), rivalry is real but manageable (Mondelez, Nestlé, Mars face the same
input-cost cycle). The moat itself is showing up in the numbers, not just the Morningstar
label: Q2 FY2026 revenue was $2.79B (+6.6% YoY, beating the Street's $2.63B estimate by 5.8%),
and EBIT of $563.5M beat estimates by 26.4% — driven substantially by pricing power (~10
points of net price realization in Q1 2026) rather than volume. That's the moat working as
designed: passing a genuine commodity shock through to the customer while competitors eat the
same cost pressure. The crack: volume fell roughly 2 points alongside that pricing, and
management's own framing of the open question is worth quoting directly — is the volume
softness "only temporary price elasticity after an extreme cocoa shock, or early evidence that
the core confectionery moat is weakening under higher prices and changing consumer behavior"?
That is exactly the kind of question pillar 1 needs a live answer to, not a one-time verdict.

**Pillar 2 (transparency) — passes, in clear contrast to Campbell's.** Hershey's ~$3B
strategic bet on salty snacks (Dot's Pretzels, SkinnyPop, LesserEvil, Pirate's Booty, Reese's
Filled Pretzels) is reported as its own standalone segment with real sales *and* income
figures every quarter — North America Salty Snacks net sales were $388M in Q2 2026 (+23%
YoY) with segment income disclosed separately ($62.5M, -5.9% YoY). This is the single
starkest difference from Campbell's Rao's situation: Hershey never folded its comparable
acquisition-led diversification bet into an opaque catch-all unit, so a clean,
checkpoint-able number exists for exactly the thing that matters.

**Track record — mixed, and instructive precisely because disclosure makes it visible.**
Salty Snacks is growing fast on the top line (+23–28% YoY across the last several quarters)
but segment income fell in Q2 2026 on freight/logistics costs and production bottlenecks that
constrained Dot's Pretzels and variety-multipack output — an operational execution problem,
not (so far) a demand problem. That's a real, live parallel to Campbell's snacks-margin
struggle, but the starting position is different in an important way: Hershey's core
confectionery business is healthy and growing, so this diversification bet isn't propping up
a declining core the way Rao's is for Campbell's — it's additive to an already-working moat,
which lowers (but does not eliminate) the stakes if the integration stumbles further.

**Forward checkpoints (set 2026-08-21, grade at Q3 FY2026, expected ~late October/early
November 2026 based on the Q2 report's July 30 date):**
1. *Salty Snacks margin recovery* — does segment income rebound as the freight/logistics and
   production-bottleneck issues cited in Q2 resolve, or does the margin pressure persist/worsen?
   This is the direct test of the "operational, not structural" read above.
2. *Core confectionery volume trend* — does the ~2-point volume decline stabilize as pricing
   laps the prior year, or does it deepen? This is the direct test of whether pillar 1's crack
   is temporary elasticity or real moat erosion.
3. *Cocoa cost guidance* — does management's stated "good visibility into cocoa cost deflation
   in 2027" actually firm up into specific guidance, or get walked back as El Niño-related
   supply risk evolves?
4. *Consensus-vs-screen convergence* — does live analyst consensus (currently Hold, ~$205–217)
   move toward Morningstar's $230 fair value as results come in, or do estimates cut further?
   A move in either direction is informative; no movement for several quarters would suggest
   the two views are pricing genuinely different scenarios, not just lagging each other.
5. *Insider activity / buybacks* — any change in pace, as an independent read on management's
   own conviction.

**Initiation verdict (2026-08-21): lean constructive, more so than Campbell's on pillar 2
specifically.** The moat is demonstrably working (real pricing power flowing through to
margin during an active commodity shock), and — unlike Campbell's — the diversification bet
is fully checkpoint-able because Hershey actually discloses it. The open question is narrow
and well-defined (temporary elasticity vs. real erosion, operational vs. structural snacks
margin pressure) rather than blocked by a disclosure gap. Not yet an INCLUDE-equivalent
conviction — the Street's own skepticism (Hold, single-digit-to-teens upside on live
consensus) is a real countervailing data point against Morningstar's more bullish screen —
but a cleaner setup than Campbell's to actually monitor and act on as checkpoints resolve.

## Campbell's vs. Hershey — direct comparison

Both are the same species of bet: a healthy-or-struggling legacy CPG core paired with a
recent, sizable, brand-led acquisition strategy, screened via Morningstar's wide-moat list.
They diverge sharply once run through the framework's two pillars rather than just compared
on screen-level discount:

**Core business health (pillar 1 starting point).** Campbell's legacy soup is declining and
commoditized — the diversification bet (Rao's) has to carry the thesis largely on its own.
Hershey's legacy confectionery is growing and demonstrating real pricing power through an
active cost shock — the diversification bet (Salty Snacks) is additive to an already-working
moat, not a rescue attempt. This is the single biggest structural difference between the two.

**Disclosure (pillar 2 — the framework's real differentiator).** This is where the two cases
diverge most starkly, and where the framework's discipline earns its keep: Campbell's folded
Rao's into an opaque "Distinctive Brands" unit, blocking the one checkpoint that matters most.
Hershey reports Salty Snacks as a clean standalone segment with sales and income every
quarter. Identical bet type, opposite transparency outcome — Hershey is checkpoint-able in a
way Campbell's currently is not.

**Track record on the acquisition-integration capability.** Both show real margin pressure in
the newly-acquired segment (Campbell's snacks at 7% margin, down 390bp; Hershey's Salty
Snacks income down 5.9% YoY on freight/production issues). Campbell's problem looks more
structural (multi-quarter, tied to an older 2018 deal); Hershey's looks more operational
(freight/logistics/production bottlenecks, disclosed in the same quarter as strong topline
growth) — but this is exactly the kind of read that needs the next 1-2 quarters of checkpoint
grading to confirm, not something to conclude from a single data point.

**Statistical cheapness vs. framework conviction — a useful divergence.** Campbell's screens
as far "cheaper" on Morningstar's own numbers (~59% implied discount to FV vs. Hershey's
~19%), and carries a lower analyst-consensus-vs-screen gap risk since Hershey's Street
consensus (Hold, ~$205-217) sits notably below Morningstar's $230 figure. Read naively, that
discount would favor Campbell's. Read through the two-pillar framework, it's closer to the
opposite: Campbell's apparent cheapness is largely a symptom of the declining legacy business
and the unresolved, unmonitorable Rao's bet, while Hershey's smaller discount comes with a
core business that's provably working and a diversification bet that's actually auditable.
Cheap-and-opaque is a worse combination than moderately-priced-and-transparent under this
framework's own logic.

**Net:** as of 2026-08-21, Hershey is the cleaner setup of the two to actually monitor and act
on, notwithstanding its smaller statistical discount — precisely because pillar 2 passes. Both
remain in "wait for checkpoints" status, not action-ready; Campbell's checkpoints resolve
first (~October 2026), Hershey's shortly after (~late October/November 2026).
